Overview

LiDAR Drone is a generative survival horror game where you remotely pilot a drone through procedurally generated mazes with one hard constraint: your only source of vision is a LiDAR scanner. The world exists only where the scan beam touches it — everything else is darkness.

The core tension isn't jump scares — it's the anxiety of incomplete information. You can hear something nearby. You know it's there. You just can't see the full picture.

// The concept

You're not a character in a dungeon — you're an operator at a terminal, receiving a signal feed from a robot drone. The terminal/CRT visual aesthetic makes the world feel like data being received from a sensor rather than a place you inhabit. The LiDAR scan renders geometry as sparse point-cloud outlines, creating an atmosphere where the technology itself becomes part of the horror.

The game draws inspiration from The Voidness — which pioneered LiDAR as a horror mechanic, revealing monsters only through scan returns — and Escape the Backrooms Together, whose procedurally generated liminal spaces and cooperative tension shaped how we thought about level design and pacing.

// Gameplay loop

  • Navigate procedurally generated maze levels, finding keys to unlock doors to the next floor
  • Scan monsters to reveal facts about them — each scan gives a random entry from a per-monster fact database, teaching you how to survive encounters
  • A persistent bestiary accumulates knowledge across runs, rewarding repeated play
  • Early monsters are forgiving and teach the mechanic; later ones require mastery to handle
  • Audio is the primary danger signal — distortions in the scan and sound cues warn you before you visually confirm a threat

// Technical highlights

  • Built the LiDAR scan visual effect as a custom shader in Godot — our first real experience writing GLSL-style shader code
  • Implemented procedural maze generation to keep each run structurally distinct
  • Designed a monster AI system with multiple behaviour profiles tuned to introduce difficulty gradually
  • Managed a larger collaborative Godot codebase across three developers, learning scene architecture and signal patterns the hard way

What I learned

This was my first serious game project and the most significant takeaway was how much game design is really about managing player information. Every decision — what the player can see, what they can hear, what the UI reveals — shapes the emotional experience independently of the underlying technology. Designing around a hard visibility constraint forced us to think carefully about every system.

On the technical side, writing a custom LiDAR shader in Godot was genuinely new territory. Rendering the world as scan data rather than solid geometry meant thinking differently about the rendering pipeline. Coming second at LiU Game Awards 2025 and presenting at East Sweden Game Summit was a great signal that the core idea resonated beyond just our course.
